Project Overview

FCI SmartCloud provides IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS services to help businesses of all sizes modernize their operations. When I joined, the product had grown organically — each feature was built separately, components weren’t shared, and the experience felt inconsistent across every screen.

The goal wasn’t to redesign the surface. The goal was to rebuild the foundation: understand what actually existed, establish the system underneath, and then make the product feel like one thing.

My first move wasn’t a redesign — it was rebuilding the sitemap and user flow against what actually existed, so any decision after that was grounded in the real product, not the documentation of it.

Team & My Role

I was the lead product designer working closely with product managers and engineering leads across two product tracks simultaneously.

My Role

Lead Product Designer

System architecture, design system, UX research, mobile app design, Figma ownership.

Product

Product Manager

Roadmap, stakeholder alignment, feature prioritization.

Engineering

Frontend Lead

Component implementation, design system integration, technical constraints.

Engineering

Backend Engineers (×3)

API design, service logic, platform infrastructure.

QA

QA Engineer

Test planning, design review for edge cases and error states.

Stakeholder

FPT / FCI Leadership

Business direction, Hackathon sponsor, final approval.

Target Audience

The platform served two distinct user types with very different needs and technical literacy levels.

Enterprise IT Admins

Technical users managing cloud resources, provisioning services, and monitoring infrastructure. They need control, precision, and fast access to system status.

Business Decision Makers

Non-technical managers evaluating cloud adoption. They need clarity on what the platform does and trust that it’s reliable — without reading a manual.

Developers

Engineers deploying and managing applications on the platform. They value efficiency, good error messaging, and predictable behavior.

Mobile Users (new segment)

Users who needed to monitor and manage their cloud resources on the go — the audience for the first mobile app I designed for this product.

Business Goals

  • Reduce onboarding friction for new enterprise clients adopting FCI cloud services
  • Create a consistent product experience that could scale across new features without design debt accumulating further
  • Expand reach to mobile users by shipping the product’s first native mobile app
  • Win the FPT Hackathon to demonstrate innovation and team capability
  • Enable the design system to be inherited and improved by future designers without rework
The business needed the product to feel enterprise-grade. The users needed it to feel usable. Those two goals were more aligned than the existing product suggested.

Design Context

The Before State

Before I joined, each feature had been built in isolation. The components used on the product were not always consistent — some screens used different button styles, spacing tokens, and navigation patterns for the same interaction. There was no shared sitemap that accurately reflected the live product.

The Constraint

We couldn’t stop shipping to refactor everything. The design system had to be built incrementally, replacing components as features were updated — not as a big-bang redesign.

My Starting Point

I audited the live product, not the documentation. Every screen, every flow, every component — mapped to understand what actually existed before deciding what to change.

User Pain Points

Pain point #1 — Navigation

Users couldn’t predict where features lived. The navigation had grown without a consistent information architecture, so mental models broke across sections.

Pain point #2 — Inconsistent components

The same action — e.g., confirming a deletion — triggered different modal patterns depending on which part of the product the user was in.

Pain point #3 — No mobile access

Users who needed to check service status or respond to alerts on the go had no mobile option — they relied entirely on desktop.

Pain point #4 — Error state confusion

Error messages were technical and often untranslated for non-developer users, leaving business decision-makers unable to self-resolve issues.

Key Metrics

1st

Native mobile app in the product’s history, shipped for iOS and Android.

Won the FPT Corporation Hackathon with the redesigned platform.

2yr

From the first audit to a design system the product now runs on.

User Research

I conducted interviews with 8 internal users (IT admins, developers, and one business manager) and reviewed support tickets to identify recurring friction patterns.

Key Insights

Finding 01 — Navigation mental model

Users grouped features by service type (compute, storage, networking), but the navigation was grouped by when features were built. The two didn’t match.

Finding 02 — Trust in error states

Non-technical users skipped error modals entirely when they didn’t understand them — they called support instead. Error copy was an invisible UX problem.

Finding 03 — Mobile monitoring behavior

3 out of 8 users said they checked service health via Slack notifications because there was no mobile view — a workaround that masked the gap in the product.

Finding 04 — De-biasing moment

I assumed denser information on the dashboard would feel more “powerful” to enterprise users. Testing showed the opposite: simpler layouts got faster task completion. I adapted the design to the simpler version.

Scope & User Stories

The project was scoped into two phases: foundation (design system + IA) and expansion (mobile app + new features).

Logic & Business Rules

Service provisioning flow

Key business rules

  • Users cannot provision services exceeding their account quota — the system must surface remaining quota before step 2, not at confirmation
  • Deletion of active services requires a typed confirmation (service name) to prevent accidental loss
  • Error states must include a plain-language explanation + recommended action for non-technical users, in addition to error codes for developers
  • Mobile app read access only — provisioning remains desktop-only in v1 to reduce complexity and risk

Outcome

The redesign delivered a unified product experience across web and mobile — the first time SmartCloud felt like a single platform rather than a collection of features.

  • Rebuilt the sitemap and navigation around how users actually think about cloud services, not how the product was built
  • Established a design system with shared tokens, components, and documentation — inheritable by future team members
  • Shipped the product’s first mobile app (iOS + Android), covering monitoring and alert management
  • Won the FPT Corporation Hackathon, demonstrating the team’s ability to ship innovative work under pressure
  • Error states rewritten in plain language, reducing support ticket volume for UI-related issues
The work that mattered most wasn’t the final screens — it was the system underneath them. Every feature after this point had a foundation to build on.

Overview

FPT Cloud Server provides a secure and reliable virtual server system, designed centrally for easy scalability. Users can create and configure compute instances with minimal operations, and control the system through the web, CLI and API. Each compute component can be configured separately: processor, storage, networking, operating system and charging method.

Product advantages

Auto-Scale

Instances resize automatically according to workload and usage needs.

Uptime 99.99%

FPT Cloud’s cloud server is committed to stability, with up to 99.99% uptime.

Safety & Security

A built-in Standard Firewall L4, plus firewall options from major security firms.

Load Balancing

Load sharing across virtual servers improves performance, availability and scalability.

My Role

I was the only designer on the product, working closely with the Product Owner team in an Agile process (Jira and Microsoft 365).

  • Collaborating with the PO team
  • Sitemaps, wireframing and prototyping
  • Design iteration and high-fidelity UI mockups
  • Building the design system
  • Marketing assets

Design Process

Research

We analysed user feedback collected by the sales teams and ran internal surveys to find the critical pain points and the areas to improve. From there, the Product Owner and I agreed on three key insights:

Insight 01

A consistent design system is needed across web and mobile.

Insight 02

Complex cloud management tasks need intuitive navigation and user flows.

Insight 03

On mobile, users need easy monitoring and quick access to critical information.

Ideation

With the research done, we built new wireframes and user flows for the web and mobile app, focusing on navigation, resource management and data visualisation.

Iterative design

Building on the research, the competitive analysis and the user flows, I created the design system and style guide that keep every product module consistent.

  • Design System, version 2.0
  • Style Guideline, version 1.0
  • Product design for desktop, mobile and tablet (UI version 2.0.0, 2024)

Problem-Solving

ChallengeSolution
Redesigning an active product without disrupting current usersA phased redesign, prioritising critical features based on user feedback
Creating a cohesive experience across complex modules (IAM, Billing, Compute Engine and more)A robust design system that keeps every module consistent
Balancing full functionality for IT professionals with ease of use for managers on mobileSeparate user flows for technical and management users, each optimised for their needs

User-Centered Design

I focused on intuitive interfaces for complex cloud management tasks, serving both technical IT staff and upper management. The mobile app is built for quick notifications, easy monitoring of virtual machines and a simple billing overview.

Technical Considerations

I worked with the development teams to make sure every design improvement was feasible within the existing system architecture, and designed with scalability in mind so new features can be added without hurting the experience.

Outcome and Impact

The project is in the planning and bug-fixing stage, with a roadmap for gradual UX/UI improvements. I created a full plan for rebuilding the design system and component library, to keep the product consistent and reduce hardcoded elements.

Lessons Learned

This project shows how I approach complex design challenges in enterprise cloud services: building a design system, balancing technical requirements with user needs, and improving a live product without disrupting the people who use it every day.

  • A solid foundation (the design system) has to come before rapid feature development
  • Redesigning an active product takes careful planning and prioritisation
  • Continuous user feedback is what guides good decisions on complex technical products

Overview

My team’s goal was a complete data visualisation solution, comparable to Power BI, to support financial and accounting work across FPT Corporation and eventually replace Power BI. The need sounded simple, but meeting it meant the PO and BA team kept reviewing Power BI’s features, picking the ones that truly mattered to the business and its subsidiaries, and researching how competitors handle big data and data security.

  • Web application: data visualisation, reports and dashboards, data modelling, account management, workspace and organisation management
  • Native mobile app: viewing reports and dashboards

My Role

I was the only designer on the product, working with the PO team in an Agile process (Jira and Microsoft 365).

  • Collaborating with the PO team
  • Sitemaps, wireframing and prototyping
  • Design iteration and high-fidelity UI mockups
  • Building the design system
  • Marketing assets

Design Process

Research

With the Product Owner’s support, I collected Power BI’s functional analytics and user needs, so I could look at the product’s UX objectively. From there, we agreed on three key insights:

Insight 01

A secure, multi-level data access system is essential.

Insight 02

Data modelling and visualisation tools have to be intuitive.

Insight 03

The product has to work across web and mobile.

Ideation

After working through the needs with the team, we completed the core user flows for the first features, then started wireframes and user flows for web and mobile, with the focus on data modelling, visualisation and account administration.

Iterative design

Finally, we built a complete design system and high-fidelity prototypes, and refined them with stakeholder feedback and A/B testing until the result worked as well as it could for the end user.

Problem-Solving

ChallengeSolution
Designing an intuitive interface for complex data modelling and visualisationA step-by-step data modelling process with clear visual cues
Creating a secure, multi-level permission system without hurting the user experienceA granular permission system with intuitive controls
Keeping web and mobile consistentA flexible design system that adapts to both web and mobile interfaces

User-Centered Design

Throughout the roadmap, we aimed for data displays that are easy to understand and pleasant to look at, so people can get insights quickly and make informed decisions. Data Suite is tailored to different roles within FPT, each with its own level of data access and analysis needs.

Technical Considerations

The engineering team made sure the architecture supported many ways of entering data and connecting databases, and worked with us to verify that real-time updates and cross-platform sync were feasible.

Outcome and Impact

When I moved to another project in March 2023, Data Suite was still a proof of concept, with selected FPT member companies trying the platform and running A/B tests to refine the experience. By FPT Techday in November 2024, the product had been released to end users and was in its marketing campaign.

Lessons Learned

This project shows my work on complex data visualisation tools: balancing user needs with strict security requirements, building a full design system and adapting it for web and mobile.

  • Simplicity and visual appeal are what turn data into insight
  • Multi-level data access is complex to design without making the experience feel heavy
  • Complex data tools need continuous testing and iteration

Overview

Pizza Hut Digital Ventures (formerly Pizza Hut Digital & Technology) is a digital brand owned by Pizza Hut, part of Yum! Brands. I’m proud to have been part of PHDV’s work on a world-class digital platform (web, iOS and Android), helping Pizza Hut grow as a digital franchisor through data-driven technology, thought partnership and a people-first culture, and giving the best ordering experience to everyone who enjoys the Pizza Hut menu.

At PHDV, I designed and improved websites and mobile apps, and partnered with other teams to build the core design system. My goal was to grow my product design knowledge across the whole product development cycle.

Team & My Role

  • Assisting UX research in Asia
  • Sitemaps, user flows, wireframing and prototyping
  • Internal testing in Vietnam
  • Design iteration and high-fidelity UI mockups
  • Assisting the design system

The product team had 100–150 people: design, UX research, web and mobile development, product managers and more, working in Agile with Jira and Monday.

In my first month, I spent a lot of time talking with the design team. Before, I had worked with one teammate and handled most of the team’s tasks; here I had international colleagues with many years of experience. I started learning standard product processes and took on specific website and app features under the direction of the Team Leader.

Business Story

Pizza Hut’s sales growth had slowed, while competitors such as Domino’s grew their digital offering. After a $130 million investment from parent company Yum! Brands, Pizza Hut Digital Ventures (now Pizza Hut Digital & Technology) was set up in 2017 to use digital technology to improve the customer experience: online payment, delivery and takeaway ordering, and loyalty programmes.

Problem 01

Sales growth dropped significantly.

Problem 02

Pizza Hut had fallen behind Domino’s and other pizza franchise competitors.

Problem 03

Placing orders and customising items was difficult.

Problem 04

Paying in person was inconvenient.

Our vision

PHDT had a clear mission and a focused product vision: one core, creative e-commerce platform, with websites and native apps for every franchise market, that gives customers an outstanding experience and makes ordering and delivery more efficient and cost-effective.

“Delivering Digital & Technology Excellence for Our Customers, Team Members, and Franchisees.”

Product Features

Website and mobile app

Customers can order their favourite pizzas from anywhere, at any time.

Delivery method & order time

Simple delivery choices and scheduling, such as “ASAP” or “schedule for later”.

Menu & item customisation

An easy online menu to browse and customise orders: toppings, crusts and sides.

Cart & payment

A user-friendly cart to manage the order, and quick, secure online payment.

Order tracking

Schedule an order for later and follow its progress in real time.

Points & rewards

A loyalty programme that rewards every online order with points for discounts, free pizzas and special offers.

Personas & Customer Journeys

After discovering and empathising, we moved to defining. The team ran usability tests with two people who matched our core audience segments: we showed them the current Pizza Hut website and asked them to order a pizza with one method, delivery or pickup. The test results showed the main difficulties:

Delivery

Ordering for delivery

Customers struggled to check which items were available on the menu and to customise their order.

Pickup

Ordering for pickup

Customers had trouble collecting their order at the store, because of how the order and customer details were checked.

The design team in Vietnam mapped the first customer journey in Q1 2019.

Target Audience

In the second round of research (Q4 2019), the UX researchers at PHDV UK ran user research and a survey with 31 participants across Japan. Synthesising the results with them and with the business strategy, we found three main age groups:

26%

Ages 30–35

Career-focused customers who order pizza for quick meals, drawn by the variety of flavours, toppings, sides and home delivery.

26%

Ages 35–40

Established professionals and managers who value deals and combos for family meals and social gatherings.

13%

Ages 40–45

Senior professionals and entrepreneurs who choose deals and combos for company meetings and social occasions.

Competitive Research

Competitive research was an essential part of the define stage. By analysing the features and solutions across the market, we saw where Pizza Hut stood, which services were basic and which were complete, and the strengths and weaknesses of our competitors. This helped us define our value proposition and find where the product could stand out.

Sitemap & User Flow

The sitemap and user flow showed the whole journey of a user in the app, from placing an order to checkout. They helped us find opportunities to improve the experience, and gave designers, product managers and developers a shared picture of how the proposed solution was structured.

Hi-fi UI Mockups (Version 2, 2020)

In 2020, as the company grew fast, the design team expanded across the UK, the US and Vietnam. It was an exciting chance to build the second version together with product designers and UX researchers around the world.

Delivery option & order time

The app supports delivery and pickup. Pickup users find nearby stores on a map, by typing a location or using their phone’s location. Delivery users enter their address by postcode or street address, then choose a preferred delivery time.

Menu & item customisation

The menu has six categories: deals, pizzas, sides, dips, desserts and drinks. Users customise a pizza by size, crust, sauce, cheese and toppings: they can create their own pizza, pick any four toppings, or go half and half with different choices on each half before adding it to the cart.

Shopping cart & checkout

In the cart, users manage the order time, delivery address and items, and can add coupons and suggested extras. At checkout, they enter the recipient’s details, choose a payment method, and can register to join the loyalty programme. After the order is placed, a confirmation screen shows order tracking, estimated delivery time, order details, address and payment method.

What I Learned

Pizza Hut Digital & Technology was a great chance to go deep into digital product design, where creative design and technology come together to build products that give customers a seamless experience and keep them engaged. It also let me work cross-functionally and support the whole product development process.

  • How digital products are developed in a franchise environment
  • Keeping data consistent across the website and mobile app
  • Collaborating cross-functionally with Agile teams
  • Working in a diverse, multicultural environment

Good Design Leaves Room to Breathe

Oct 19, 2026 4 min readUX & Product Design

I used to think a good design was one where every detail had a purpose, every space was filled, and every interaction had something interesting to offer. Over time, I began to appreciate the opposite: sometimes, the best thing I can do for a design is to leave something out.

White space gives content room to breathe. A quiet interface lets people focus on what matters instead of asking them to process everything at once. I think of it as being a thoughtful host — making people feel comfortable without constantly trying to get their attention.

These days, I try to ask myself one simple question before adding anything new: Does this make the experience better, or does it simply make the screen busier?

Design Diary · No. 03

The Small Details That Make UX Feel Human

Oct 23, 2026 5 min readUX & Product Design

When we talk about user experience, we often discuss flows, usability, accessibility, and conversion rates. These things matter, of course. But I believe the emotional side of an experience often lives in much smaller details.

Think about a form that remembers your previous choices, an error message that explains how to fix a problem, or a confirmation screen that tells you exactly what will happen next. None of these features needs to look impressive. They simply make people feel less lost.

For me, good UX is not about making users admire the interface. It is about helping them feel capable while using it. The best experience might be the one they barely notice — because everything works as they expected.

Painting has taught me to look at ordinary things a little longer. The shadow beneath a cup, the uneven edge of a leaf, the way afternoon light changes the color of a wall — details I might have overlooked suddenly become interesting.

Design Diary · No. 05

Design Is Not Just About What We Add

Oct 27, 2026 5 min readUX & Product Design

One of the hardest lessons in product design is learning that more features do not automatically create more value.

A product team may ask for another filter, another shortcut, or another section on an already crowded page. Each request might make sense on its own. But when everything becomes a priority, the experience can become harder to understand.

I have learned to step back and ask three questions: What problem are we solving? Who experiences this problem? And is there a simpler way to solve it?

Constraints can be uncomfortable, especially when a layout has limited space or a requirement seems impossible to avoid. But constraints can also encourage better thinking. Sometimes, the most meaningful design decision is not what we introduce — it is what we choose to leave behind.

If animals could review our interfaces, I wonder what they would say.

Perhaps they would ask why a button is so small, why the important thing is hidden behind three menus, or why a simple action requires so many steps. They would probably care very little about our beautiful gradients and carefully chosen typefaces.

I think there is a small UX lesson here: before making something impressive, make sure it works for the one who needs it. Whether designing for people or caring for animals, empathy begins when we stop assuming that everyone experiences the world the same way we do.

There is a particular kind of patience that crochet teaches you. When a stitch goes wrong, you can pretend not to notice and keep going. But sooner or later, the mistake finds its way into the shape of the finished piece.

So you stop. You look at your work. And sometimes, you unravel several rows to correct one small detail.

I used to see starting over as wasted effort. Now, I think of it as part of making something with care. In design, too, the first version is rarely the final one. Research changes our assumptions. Testing reveals unexpected problems. Feedback gives us a reason to reconsider.

The trick is not to avoid mistakes. It is to notice them early enough, understand why they happened, and have the patience to make things better.

As designers, we often talk about color through systems: primary and secondary palettes, semantic colors, contrast ratios, and accessibility guidelines. These foundations are essential, but my personal relationship with color begins somewhere less technical.

Designing With More Kindness

Nov 6, 2026 5 min readUX & Product Design

The longer I work in design, the more I believe that good design begins with paying attention to people.

Not just the users described in research documents, but the real people behind those descriptions. The person who is tired after a long day. Someone who is unfamiliar with technology. A customer who is worried about making a mistake. A colleague who is trying to explain a complicated requirement.

Kindness in design is not about making every screen soft or every message cheerful. Sometimes, it means giving clear instructions. Sometimes, it means allowing people to correct an error without starting over. Sometimes, it means protecting their time by removing an unnecessary step.

I want to keep learning the technical side of my craft, but I also want to remember why I do it. The goal is not simply to make digital products look better. It is to make everyday interactions a little easier, clearer, and more considerate.

Perhaps that is the kind of designer I want to become: someone who pays attention, asks better questions, and leaves people with a little less frustration than they came with.
